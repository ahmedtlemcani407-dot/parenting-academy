Last Tuesday my kids fought over the blue cup. Then the red cup. Then who got to sit on the left side of the sofa, which is not meaningfully different from the right side of the sofa. By 11am I had intervened eleven times and I was keeping count, which tells you everything about my state of mind.

I used to treat every fight like a court case. Who started it. Whose turn it was. Who was holding the toy first, with witness testimony from a four-year-old. I thought I was teaching fairness. What I was actually teaching was that fighting is the fastest way to get a parent's full attention, and that justice is whatever Mom or Dad declares while sighing.

The courtroom approach had to go. Here's what replaced it.

First: I stopped solving it. When the shouting starts and nobody is bleeding, I say one sentence — "sounds like you two need to figure this out" — and I leave the room. The first week was awful. The fights got louder, like they were performing for an audience that had walked out. Then, slowly, they started negotiating. Badly. A four-year-old's negotiation tactics are mostly yelling "fine, YOU have it" and storming off. But it was theirs, not mine, and the frequency dropped because the payoff — a parent rushing in — was gone.

Second: I separated them before the courtroom, not after. Most of our fights happened in the same danger windows: tired, hungry, or stuck inside too long. Now when I hear the pre-fight whine pitch, I don't wait for the crime. One goes to the garden, one stays inside. It's not a punishment, I don't announce it as one, and I don't care who "started it." Distance is the whole intervention.

Third: I gave them a repair script for later, when everyone's calm. "You were mad. Next time, say 'I was using that' instead of grabbing." We practice it the way you'd practice anything with small kids — clumsily, repeatedly, while they giggle. It works about half the time, which is roughly double the old rate.

What didn't work: forced sharing on a timer ("two more minutes each" — they spent the minutes glaring), making them apologize to each other (empty words, extra resentment), and the big one — asking "who started it." There is never a satisfactory answer to that question. There is only a longer fight about the fight.

The part nobody warns you about: some of it is just developmental. Kids under six are terrible at sharing, turn-taking, and emotional regulation, and putting two of them in one house is a contact sport. It doesn't mean they hate each other. Mine fight like rivals and then conspire against me at bedtime like business partners. Both things are true.

We're down from forty fights to maybe fifteen on a bad day. I'll take it.
